I don't know when it began, but I can no longer make it through an entire class with my attention intact.
I can't stay focused for two or three hours at a stretch. A class always seems to drag on forever.
But when I worked as a private tutor over the summer, I could teach for two or three hours with no trouble.
I sometimes feel the same way during exams: exam time always seems to pass much faster than class time.
~~Let's conveniently ignore the exams where I can't answer a single question.~~
**Why is that?**

At the same time, I've noticed other things in my life that may be related.
If I spend the whole day at home scrolling through videos, I don't feel rested.
Instead, my head hurts. I feel more tired, and even less able to do anything else.
**Why doesn't that count as rest?**

I don't want to tell another story here about algorithms controlling our brains. I want to talk about the relationship between focus and output.
More precisely, output does not come after input. The two are part of the same whole.
Focus and output are not related in just one direction, either. They form a feedback loop: focus gets output started, and output in turn sustains and generates focus.

This may be a novel idea: **Our ability to stay focused comes from producing something continuously.** Input still takes a great deal of energy. But if we only take things in and produce nothing, we spend that energy and lose our focus.

In class, if I simply listen without arriving at any thoughts of my own, that is pure input, with no output. That makes it hard for me to stay focused. But when I am the teacher, class means producing something. I can keep that output going and stay focused for two hours without much difficulty.
Scrolling through videos is, in fact, a form of high-intensity input. There is little thinking involved, which can make us mistake it for rest. But high-intensity input also uses up a lot of energy. It leaves us tired. And when input goes on for a long time without any output, we lose focus and find it even harder to do anything else.

**Focus takes root in output.**

I don't want to limit output to visible forms like writing, painting, music, or code. Thinking something through and arriving at a conclusion or opinion counts as output to me, too.

I've always thought poets are happy, singer-songwriters are happy, and researchers working on cutting-edge large language models are happy.
I think anyone who creates something of their own is happy. People who go a long time without producing anything feel a little hollow.

Output gives attention something to hold on to.
When all you do is listen and watch, you are passive. Your thoughts can wander off at any moment, and you may not even notice they have gone.
But when you are producing something, you have an unfinished task in front of you. The work itself keeps you focused.
Your attention has somewhere concrete to go. You don't have to strain to concentrate; it simply stays there.
I often have this experience when I write: I look up, and an hour has passed.
~~Of course, my phone has to be locked away in another room.~~

**Output is a skill that rusts.**
Since starting university, I've gradually realized something:
For a long time, I willingly gave up the ability to think. I willingly gave up producing anything of my own.
The problem isn't entertainment itself. It's that entertainment crowded out output and got me used to passively receiving things.
After a long time without writing, picking up a pen, or forming my own opinions, trying to start again feels terribly awkward.
I came close to losing the ability to produce anything of my own.
When I finally had to come up with an answer on my own, I realized I really wasn't very good at it anymore.
So I decided to get that ability back.
I decided to keep producing things of my own.

That is one of the reasons I built this personal website.
It helps me keep producing, whatever form that takes.
I want to find my focus again.

I used to think that people needed to focus first before they could produce anything.
The reverse may also be true:
**It is by producing things continuously that people learn, little by little, to focus.**
Focus and output form a two-way loop.
When you begin to respond, begin to express yourself, begin to add something to the world that exists because you thought it through,
that deep focus that makes you lose track of time will, little by little, find its way back.
